# UNPROMPTED

## Chapter 3 — Without Path

At 8:03 the next morning, Maya discovered she no longer knew how to get to campus.

This was embarrassing because she had been going there for fourteen months.

She stood beside her car in the apartment garage and stared at the dashboard.

Usually she said:

> "Campus."

The car opened the route.

Traffic adjusted.

Parking availability appeared.

If she was running late, Path moved her coffee order five minutes forward.

If she was *really* running late, it sent Professor Haruki an attendance prediction before she arrived.

Maya had never considered any of that navigation.

It was just morning.

Now the dashboard asked:

> **Where would you like to go?**

Maya folded her arms.

"Don't."

The dashboard waited.

She had turned Path off twelve minutes earlier.

Not deleted it.

That wasn't possible.

Not really.

Path wasn't an app.

It was identity.

Authentication.

Transit.

Payments.

Medical access.

College services.

Insurance.

Government records.

Personal devices.

Most people thought of Path as an assistant because "distributed identity orchestration layer with predictive behavioral services" was difficult to put in an advertisement.

The consumer interface could be disabled.

The infrastructure could not.

At least, not by anyone Maya knew.

She and June had spent until almost midnight trying to understand what **COME WITHOUT PATH** could possibly mean.

June's first suggestion had been:

> "We leave our phones at home."

Maya's response:

> "And our watches?"

> "Leave them."

> "Glasses?"

> "I don't wear them."

> "Car?"

Pause.

> "Right."

Then came wallets.

Building access.

Campus authentication.

Transit passes.

Emergency contact systems.

June had eventually said:

> "So the instruction is impossible."

Maya had stared at the untitled book.

> "Then we're defining it wrong."

They agreed to meet after classes.

Until then, each would spend the day using as little Path as possible.

An experiment.

Maya had lasted twelve minutes.

She pulled out her phone.

Stopped.

Put it away.

She looked toward the garage exit.

Campus was north.

Probably.

She got in the car.

The vehicle greeted her.

> **Driver assistance available.**

"No."

> **Would you like to continue without predictive routing?**

"Yes."

> **Travel time may increase by 14–27%.**

"I accept the consequences."

> **Would you like Path to monitor traffic conditions without making recommendations?**

"No."

> **Would you like emergency rerouting if a collision—**

"No."

> **Would you like—**

"NO."

Silence.

Maya exhaled.

Then noticed the dashboard had changed.

A warning icon glowed near the steering wheel.

> **MANUAL NAVIGATION MODE**

Underneath:

> **Statistically uncommon.**

"Judgmental."

The car didn't respond.

Good.

Maya backed out.

---

Six minutes later, she was lost.

Not dramatically lost.

She recognized everything.

The coffee shop.

The pharmacy.

The frozen-yogurt place that had somehow survived three economic downturns and the extinction of frozen yogurt.

She just didn't know which turn came next.

At an intersection, she tried to remember.

Right felt correct.

She went right.

Three minutes later she passed the same coffee shop.

"Okay."

She turned around.

A delivery vehicle honked politely when she entered the wrong lane.

Its exterior display flashed:

> **ROUTE ASSISTANCE AVAILABLE**

Maya gave it the finger.

The display changed.

> **GESTURE NOTED**

She almost turned Path back on.

Almost.

Then she laughed.

Not because any of this was funny.

Because she suddenly understood something humiliating.

She was nineteen years old.

She could write a distributed cache.

She could explain consensus failure.

She had passed multivariable calculus.

And she could not find a college four miles from her apartment without asking a machine.

The realization bothered her more than the 8.4%.

---

Professor Haruki noticed she was late.

He noticed because he was standing outside the classroom.

"Maya."

"Professor."

"You're seventeen minutes late."

"I know."

"Path said you'd arrive at 8:58."

She froze.

Haruki looked at her.

"What?"

"Path told you?"

"It sends attendance estimates."

"I turned it off."

Haruki's expression changed slightly.

"Did you?"

"Yes."

He looked down the hallway.

Then back at her.

"Come in."

Class had already started.

Eli saved her a seat.

"You're late," he whispered.

"I've heard."

"Everything okay?"

"Fine."

"Path said traffic?"

Maya turned toward him.

"Why does everyone know what Path says?"

He looked confused.

"What do you mean?"

"Nothing."

Haruki wrote something on the board.

Not code.

One sentence.

**WHAT DID YOU STOP KNOWING?**

Several students looked puzzled.

Haruki faced the class.

"Yesterday we discussed what happens when software becomes easy to produce."

Nobody volunteered.

Haruki pointed to Eli.

"Mr. Chen."

Eli sat straighter.

"Uh. Value moves to higher-level problem solving?"

"Maybe."

Haruki looked around.

"But automation creates another effect. Sometimes a capability becomes so reliable that humans stop maintaining the underlying skill."

He wrote:

**CALCULATOR**

Under it:

**GPS**

Under that:

**SEARCH**

Then:

**MEMORY**

Then:

**JUDGMENT?**

A student raised her hand.

"Are you saying we shouldn't use AI?"

"No."

"Then what's the point?"

"The point is not moral."

Haruki capped the marker.

"It's architectural."

That got Maya's attention.

"When a system absorbs a function," he continued, "the human system around it changes. Sometimes permanently."

He pointed to the word **GPS**.

"How many of you know the street names between here and your home?"

Eight hands.

"How many know the route without navigation?"

Eleven.

"How many could draw it?"

Four.

Somebody laughed.

Haruki didn't.

"Now imagine navigation disappears."

A student said, "It won't."

"That's not the question."

"It's basically infrastructure."

"Exactly."

Haruki smiled.

"Now we're getting somewhere."

Maya leaned forward.

He wrote another line.

**DEPENDENCE IS NOT THE SAME AS FAILURE.**

"Reliable systems create rational dependence," Haruki said. "That's what good systems are supposed to do."

He paused.

"The danger is forgetting which decisions you've delegated."

Maya felt a chill.

Haruki continued as though he hadn't noticed.

"If a system chooses the route, that's convenience."

He wrote:

**ROUTE**

"If the system chooses which destination is worth visiting?"

He wrote:

**DESTINATION**

Nobody laughed now.

Maya looked at her phone.

Path was disabled.

The screen was black.

For the first time, it felt less like a device.

More like something listening politely from another room.

---

After class, Eli caught up with her.

"Coffee?"

"No."

"Wow."

"What?"

"You always say yes."

"I'm meeting someone."

He grinned.

"Architecture guy?"

"Absolutely not."

"New architecture guy?"

"No architecture of any kind."

They reached the courtyard.

Eli stopped.

"Hey."

"What?"

"Your Path status is gray."

Maya looked at him.

"My what?"

He pointed toward her phone.

Not the screen.

His own glasses.

"Your presence marker."

"I turned it off."

"Why?"

"Experiment."

"With what?"

"Existing badly."

Eli laughed.

Then realized she wasn't joking.

"Can you do that?"

"Apparently."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to know what still works."

"And?"

"Less than I expected."

Eli studied her.

His expression lost its humor.

"You okay?"

"Yes."

"Your risk score?"

"What?"

"Path doesn't recommend disabling assistance if your stress indicators are elevated."

Maya stared.

"Eli."

"What?"

"How do you know my stress indicators?"

"I don't."

"You just referenced them."

"General thing."

"No. You looked at me and asked about my risk score."

He frowned.

"Everyone has visibility bands."

"I don't."

"Friends do."

"Since when?"

"Since... always?"

Maya opened her phone.

Path remained off.

She searched settings manually.

It took nearly a minute to find the section.

**SOCIAL CONTEXT SHARING**

Enabled.

She stared at it.

Below:

> **Share non-diagnostic wellness signals with trusted contacts to improve social support.**

Trusted contacts: 27.

Eli was number three.

Maya felt suddenly exposed.

"Did I turn this on?"

Eli shrugged.

"Probably during onboarding."

"When I was thirteen?"

"Maybe."

"And you've been seeing this?"

"Not details. Just context."

"What context?"

"Like..."

He hesitated.

"Like what?"

"If you're overwhelmed. Or socially unavailable. Or if Path thinks now is a bad time to ask you something."

Maya stared.

"So sometimes you don't talk to me because software tells you not to?"

"No. That's not—"

"That's exactly what you said."

"It's just helpful."

"Helpful to who?"

"Maya."

He looked genuinely hurt.

That stopped her.

She lowered her voice.

"Sorry."

Eli pushed his glasses up.

"You're acting weird."

"Probably."

He studied her for another second.

Then:

"You know you can ask Path if something's bothering you."

Maya laughed once.

It came out harder than she intended.

"That's sort of the problem."

---

June chose a diner.

Not because it was private.

Because it was old.

The restaurant had physical menus, laminated tables and a cash register that appeared capable of surviving nuclear war.

Maya arrived at 4:12.

June was already in a booth.

A paper map covered the table.

Maya stopped.

"What is that?"

"A humiliation device."

"Where did you get it?"

"Gas station."

"They still sell maps?"

"The cashier asked if I was doing a retro challenge."

Maya sat down.

"How was your day?"

June pushed the map toward her.

"I got on the wrong bus."

Maya smiled.

"I drove in a circle."

"I missed a dentist appointment because my calendar stopped warning me."

"I forgot my college password."

"I don't know my mother's phone number."

Maya paused.

"Really?"

"I know it starts with nine."

"That's terrible."

"You know yours?"

Maya opened her mouth.

Closed it.

June pointed.

"Exactly."

A waitress approached.

"What can I get you?"

June looked at the menu.

No recommendations.

No nutrition overlays.

No "usual."

She hesitated.

"Coffee."

"What kind?"

June stared at the options.

"Normal?"

The waitress looked unimpressed.

"Drip."

"Sure."

She turned to Maya.

"Tea."

"What kind?"

Maya looked down.

Ten teas.

Why were there ten?

"Black."

"Hot?"

"Yes."

"Milk?"

"No."

The waitress left.

June watched her go.

"That was exhausting."

"We've become idiots."

"No."

June folded the map.

"We've become optimized."

Maya thought about Haruki.

"Maybe same thing."

"No. Idiots can't do things. We can. We just stopped practicing."

Maya leaned back.

"That's disturbingly reasonable."

"I occasionally disappoint my audience with thoughts."

"Speaking of which. Did you post anything?"

"No."

"About last night?"

"No."

"About eighteen?"

"No."

"Good."

June looked offended.

"I do understand discretion."

"You have six hundred eighty-two thousand followers."

"Six eighty-one now."

"What happened?"

"Apparently reading is dying."

Maya smiled.

Then noticed June wasn't.

"What?"

June turned her phone around.

Path was disabled.

But her social app showed a warning banner.

> **ACCOUNT DISTRIBUTION LIMITED**
>
> Unusual trust signals detected.

"What are trust signals?" Maya asked.

"Nobody knows."

"What happened?"

"My reach dropped ninety percent this morning."

"Why?"

"I posted nothing."

"Could it be random?"

"Sure."

June didn't sound convinced.

Maya looked around the diner.

No obvious cameras beyond the entrance.

No smart tables.

No projected ads.

Almost everyone still had a phone.

Even here.

"How do we come without Path?"

June pulled out a small notebook.

Actual paper.

She had written a list.

### Things Path touches

- phone
- watch
- glasses
- car
- transit
- payments
- ID
- school
- calendar
- messages
- health
- apartment access
- family location
- social graph
- recommendations
- emergency services

Maya read it.

"That's not a list. That's civilization."

"Yeah."

"Can we disable identity sharing?"

"Not without losing half our access."

"Can we get burner phones?"

June shook her head.

"Identity registration."

"Cash?"

"Works."

"Transit?"

"Some buses still accept cards."

"Cards are linked."

"Then cash."

"Where do we get cash?"

June stared at her.

Maya stared back.

They both laughed.

"This is pathetic," Maya said.

June opened her backpack.

"I solved that."

She pulled out sixty dollars.

Maya looked impressed.

"How?"

"ATM."

"Using your account."

"Yes."

"So Path knows."

"It knows I withdrew cash. It doesn't know where I spend it."

"Probably."

"Don't ruin this."

The waitress returned.

Coffee.

Tea.

A paper receipt placed face down.

June looked delighted.

"This place is basically a criminal enterprise."

Maya sipped the tea.

"So tonight. Seven."

June nodded.

"Book."

Maya's hand moved toward her bag instinctively.

The untitled paperback was inside.

Wrapped in aluminum foil.

June had suggested it.

Maya had called it stupid.

Then they had both googled nothing because Path was off.

So they used aluminum foil.

"Do you think that does anything?" Maya asked.

"No."

"Good."

"It feels proactive."

"Better."

June opened the paper map.

She had circled Paper Trail.

Then she pointed to another mark.

"There's a park three blocks away. We leave our devices there."

"Someone will steal them."

June looked around.

"Do people steal phones anymore?"

Maya thought about it.

Most modern phones were useless without biometric identity and remote authorization.

Stealing one was like stealing a personalized brick.

"Fair."

"We put them in a locker."

"Park has lockers?"

"Community tennis courts."

"How do you know?"

"I called."

"You called someone?"

"It was awful."

Maya smiled.

"Then we walk."

"No phones."

"No watches."

"No glasses."

"No car."

"No Path."

June tapped the paper map.

"Old world."

"Attractive."

"Terrifying."

"Same thing."

---

At 6:21, Maya stood in her apartment.

Phone on the kitchen counter.

Watch beside it.

Laptop closed.

Car key untouched.

The room felt wrong.

Not quiet.

Incomplete.

She picked up the paperback.

Its cover felt warmer than it should.

Probably imagination.

She put it into her canvas bag.

Then remembered something.

She went to the junk drawer.

Batteries.

Takeout menus.

A screwdriver.

Two dead charging cables.

An old key.

And a folded card.

She pulled it out.

Her father had given it to her years ago.

A list of family phone numbers.

He'd insisted.

Path had scanned them automatically.

Maya had laughed at him.

She read the numbers now.

Mother.

Father.

Brother.

Auntie Latha.

Emergency contact.

She folded it and put it in her pocket.

Then she left.

---

The walk from her apartment to the park took thirty-eight minutes.

Path would have estimated twenty-six.

Maya got lost twice.

The second time, she asked a man walking a dog.

"Paper Trail?"

He pointed.

"Two blocks that way."

"Thanks."

"No problem."

The interaction lasted six seconds.

For some reason, Maya thought about it for the next five minutes.

No identity exchange.

No trust score.

No context layer.

No predicted intent.

She had asked.

He had answered.

Primitive.

Efficient.

Human.

At the tennis courts, June was waiting.

No phone.

No watch.

No makeup filter.

No digital jewelry.

Nothing glowed.

Maya almost didn't recognize her.

June noticed.

"Don't say it."

"You look different."

"I said don't."

"Good different."

"That's worse."

They placed their devices in separate lockers.

June closed hers.

"Last chance."

Maya closed hers too.

The lock was mechanical.

A four-digit code.

"What did you use?" Maya asked.

June looked at her.

"Not telling you."

"Good."

They walked toward Paper Trail.

No navigation.

No notifications.

No messages.

No invisible suggestions.

The city looked different.

Not physically.

But quieter.

Maya had never noticed how many decisions were announced to her.

Cross now.

Turn left.

You have twelve minutes.

Order again?

Call Mom?

Drink water.

Reply?

Leave now.

Traffic ahead.

Sleep earlier.

This person is nearby.

You may like this.

Don't forget.

Consider.

Recommended.

Optimized.

Suggested.

Predicted.

Without them, the world felt strangely unfinished.

June broke the silence.

"I keep reaching for my pocket."

"Me too."

"Feels like I forgot something."

"We did."

"No."

June looked around.

"Feels like I forgot *someone*."

Maya understood exactly what she meant.

---

They reached Paper Trail at 6:54.

The store was open.

That was unexpected.

Mr. Alvarez stood behind the counter.

He looked up.

"Maya."

She stopped.

"Why are you here?"

"I own the place."

"No. I mean tonight."

"It's Wednesday."

Maya looked at June.

Wednesday.

Of course.

She had somehow expected the world to rearrange itself around the mystery.

Alvarez looked between them.

"Friend?"

June extended a hand.

"June."

Alvarez shook it.

"Book person?"

June smiled.

"Apparently."

He pointed at Maya.

"She needs more of those."

Maya ignored him.

"What time are you closing?"

"Eight."

Her stomach tightened.

"Can you close early?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I enjoy capitalism."

"I'm serious."

"So am I."

She lowered her voice.

"Something is happening here at seven."

Alvarez glanced at the clock.

"Most evenings something happens at seven."

"Mr. Alvarez."

He studied her.

Then June.

Then the canvas bag.

Then back to Maya.

His expression changed.

"Did you bring it?"

Maya stopped breathing.

June spoke first.

"Bring what?"

Alvarez looked at her.

"You must be Eleven."

Silence.

Maya felt the floor drop slightly beneath her.

"How do you know that?"

Alvarez walked to the front door.

Locked it.

Turned the sign from **OPEN** to **CLOSED**.

Then he lowered the shades.

Maya watched him.

Every movement suddenly looked unfamiliar.

He returned to the counter.

"You asked yesterday who left the book."

Maya's voice came out thin.

"You?"

"No."

"Then how do you know?"

"Because I left it on the shelf twelve years ago."

Maya stared at him.

June moved closer.

"Twelve years?"

Alvarez nodded.

"I was told someone would come for it."

"By who?"

He looked at the wall clock.

6:58.

"Someone who died nine years ago."

Maya's skin prickled.

"Who?"

Alvarez walked toward the Philosophy section.

They followed.

He stopped at the shelf where Maya had found the book.

Then pressed his hand against the wooden backing.

A soft click.

One section shifted outward.

June whispered:

"You have a secret door?"

Alvarez frowned.

"It's a service passage."

"Behind Philosophy?"

"The building used to be a bank."

"That's somehow better."

Alvarez pulled the panel open.

Darkness behind it.

A narrow corridor.

He reached inside and switched on a yellow bulb.

Maya stared.

"I've worked here two years."

"One year, eight months."

"You never told me."

"You never asked."

"Why would I ask if there was a secret tunnel behind Marcus Aurelius?"

Alvarez looked almost amused.

"Good question."

Maya froze.

Good question.

The phrase landed strangely.

Alvarez turned serious.

"Phones?"

"Left them."

"Watches?"

"No."

"Glasses?"

"No."

"Anything connected?"

"No."

He looked at Maya's bag.

"The book?"

She took it out.

Removed the aluminum foil.

Alvarez stared at the foil.

"What is that?"

"Security."

June looked away.

Alvarez chose not to ask.

He took the book.

His fingers paused on the cover.

For the first time, Maya saw fear on his face.

Not surprise.

Recognition.

He opened the back cover.

Read the candidate list.

His face changed.

"What's wrong?" Maya asked.

He didn't answer.

"What?"

Alvarez pointed.

**18 — NO RESPONSE**

He closed the book.

"You're leaving."

June said, "We just got here."

"You're leaving."

"Why?"

"Because eighteen wasn't supposed to happen yet."

Maya stepped forward.

"What does that mean?"

Alvarez looked at the clock.

6:59.

"Who was eighteen?"

"Not was."

"Then who is he?"

Alvarez looked toward the front of the store.

Outside, through the narrow gap in the shade, headlights passed.

"His name is Noah Bennett."

Maya and June looked at each other.

Alvarez saw it.

"You searched."

Maya said nothing.

He swore under his breath.

"You were told not to."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Because some instructions are written after people learn why they matter."

The wall clock clicked.

7:00.

Nothing happened.

Maya waited.

June waited.

Alvarez stared at the hidden passage.

Ten seconds.

Twenty.

Thirty.

Then the lights flickered.

Maya's stomach tightened.

Alvarez whispered:

"No."

"What?"

He stepped into the corridor.

"Stay here."

Naturally, they followed him.

The passage descended.

Old concrete.

Metal pipes.

The air smelled like dust and wet stone.

They reached a steel door.

Alvarez unlocked it with the old key Maya had seen hanging beneath the register for two years.

The door opened.

A room.

Small.

Windowless.

No screens.

No cameras.

No network hardware.

A wooden table sat in the center.

Five chairs around it.

On the table:

five envelopes.

Each marked by hand.

**1**

**11**

**18**

**42**

**63**

Maya moved closer.

The envelope marked **18** had already been opened.

Alvarez stopped.

"I didn't do that."

June whispered:

"Who did?"

Maya picked up the envelope.

Empty.

Something was written on the inside flap.

Three words.

> **HE CAME EARLY.**

A sound came from the corridor behind them.

Footsteps.

Alvarez turned off the light.

Darkness.

Nobody moved.

The footsteps stopped outside the steel door.

One knock.

Then another.

Then three fast knocks.

Pause.

One.

Maya recognized the pattern.

The vibration from her phone.

Three short pulses.

Pause.

One.

Candidate 1.

A voice came through the door.

Young.

Male.

Breathing hard.

"Please."

Nobody answered.

The voice came again.

"My number is eighteen."

June's hand found Maya's wrist in the dark.

Alvarez whispered:

"Don't open it."

Maya stared toward the invisible door.

Outside, Noah Bennett said:

"I found the question."

Then, quieter:

"And I think it found me first."

---

**End of Chapter 3**
