# UNPROMPTED

## Chapter 1 — Eight Point Four Percent

At 8:17 on a Tuesday morning, Maya Rao learned that her future had three years left to live.

She was standing behind the register at Paper Trail Books, holding a blueberry muffin in one hand and her phone in the other.

The muffin survived.

Her future did not.

The notification occupied the entire screen.

> **PATH CAREER ADVISORY**
>
> **Software Engineering — Entry-Level**
>
> Projected human demand, 2035: **8.4%**
>
> Current trajectory: **TERMINAL AUTOMATION**
>
> Recommended action available.

Maya read it twice.

Then she took a bite of the muffin.

"That's rude," she said.

"What is?"

Mr. Alvarez was opening a cardboard box on the floor beside the register. He owned Paper Trail, although according to Maya the more accurate description was that Paper Trail owned Mr. Alvarez.

He was sixty-three, wore the same three flannel shirts in rotation, and had spent thirty-eight years refusing to install self-checkout.

"My career just died."

"Congratulations."

Maya looked at him.

He pulled a hardcover from the box.

"Most people have to wait until they're forty-five."

"I'm serious."

"So am I."

She turned the phone toward him.

He squinted at it without moving closer.

"Eight point four percent," Maya said.

"Is that good?"

"No."

"Better than zero."

"That's your comfort?"

"My comfort is coffee. That's arithmetic."

Maya lowered the phone.

Outside the bookstore window, downtown San Ramon was beginning its daily simulation of importance.

Autonomous delivery carts rolled along the sidewalk.

A municipal cleaning drone floated past, saw a gum wrapper, descended, swallowed it and continued toward the intersection.

Across the street, the old Bank of America building had become something called **LIVE/LRN**, where people paid monthly memberships to attend classes generated specifically for their personality profiles.

A teenager walked past wearing transparent glasses.

His lips moved.

Nobody was with him.

Probably Path.

Everybody talked to Path.

Maya did too.

That wasn't strange.

Not talking to it was strange.

Her phone vibrated again.

> **Would you like Path to redesign your career trajectory?**

Two buttons appeared.

> **YES — OPTIMIZE**
>
> **NOT NOW**

Maya stared at them.

"What's it recommending?" Alvarez asked.

"I haven't clicked."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"You've clicked everything it recommended since I've known you."

"That's not true."

"Last month it told you to stop drinking oat milk."

"I stopped."

"It told you to sleep twenty-three minutes earlier."

"I feel better."

"It told you not to date that architecture guy."

Maya frowned.

"That was objectively correct."

"Then click."

Her thumb hovered above **YES — OPTIMIZE**.

She had used Path since she was thirteen.

Everyone her age had.

The federal version had originally been introduced as a student guidance system: tutoring, mental-health triage, college planning, financial literacy, career counseling.

By the time Maya reached high school, Path could negotiate cellphone plans, dispute medical bills, choose electives, compare scholarships, draft breakup texts and tell you whether the breakup was statistically premature.

At seventeen, Path recommended she study computer science.

> **Strong systems reasoning.**  
> **High abstraction tolerance.**  
> **Above-average debugging persistence.**  
> **Projected compensation: excellent.**  
> **Automation resistance: high.**

That last line had aged badly.

Maya pressed **NOT NOW**.

The screen disappeared.

For exactly half a second.

Then it returned.

> **Reason for postponement?**

"None of your business."

Path displayed three dots.

Then:

> **Technically, it is.**

Maya laughed despite herself.

Alvarez looked over.

"Your machine making jokes again?"

"It says my life is its business."

"That's not a joke."

The front door chimed.

A girl came in, maybe sixteen, followed by her mother.

They went immediately to the table marked **BOOKTOK MADE ME BUY IT**, which Alvarez pretended to hate and restocked twice a week.

Maya slipped her phone into her pocket.

She had class in forty-three minutes.

Data Systems.

The irony had already occurred to her.

---

Professor Haruki was five minutes late.

Nobody complained because nobody was actually waiting.

Twenty-seven students sat in the lecture hall.

Four were talking to each other.

Eight were watching something through retinal displays.

Three appeared asleep.

The rest were doing assignments that Path had probably already completed and that they were now rewriting to satisfy the college's Human Contribution Policy.

Maya sat beside Eli Chen.

He was looking at a blank screen.

That was unusual.

Eli's screens were never blank.

"You okay?" Maya asked.

"No."

"Exam?"

"Worse."

"Parents?"

"Worse."

"Relationship?"

"Worse."

She leaned closer.

"What?"

He rotated his laptop.

The notification looked familiar.

> **PATH CAREER ADVISORY**
>
> **Software Engineering — Entry-Level**
>
> Projected human demand, 2035: **8.4%**
>
> Current trajectory: **TERMINAL AUTOMATION**

Maya slowly pulled out her phone.

"Seriously?"

"You too?"

She showed him.

Eli stared at both screens.

"Maybe everybody got it."

"Probably."

"We should check."

He opened the class channel.

Before he could type, messages began appearing.

> **are you guys seeing this???**
>
> **8.4 LOL**
>
> **bro we paid tuition for a historical reenactment**
>
> **my mom is going to kill me before AI gets the chance**

Someone posted an image of a gravestone:

> **HERE LIES COMPUTER SCIENCE**  
> **1940–2035**  
> **IT AUTOMATED ITSELF**

Thirty laughing reactions appeared.

Maya laughed too.

Then stopped.

"Do you think it's real?"

Eli shrugged.

"It's Path."

That answered the question.

Path was wrong frequently enough about small things to be charming.

It told Maya once that a Thai restaurant was open when it wasn't.

It predicted rain on a cloudless afternoon.

It had recommended a documentary she hated so intensely she rated it one star and then spent twenty minutes explaining why.

But Path almost never made major predictions casually.

Career forecasts weren't generated by asking a chatbot what it thought.

They integrated federal labor projections, corporate hiring feeds, payroll information, education enrollment, venture investment, automation benchmarks and hundreds of other datasets Maya didn't understand.

Eight point four percent was not an opinion.

It was a warning label.

Eli leaned back.

"Well."

"Well what?"

"We pivot."

"To what?"

"I don't know. Whatever it recommends."

He tapped **YES — OPTIMIZE**.

Maya watched.

A progress indicator appeared.

> **Analyzing 18,441 possible career trajectories...**

Three seconds later:

> **PRIMARY RECOMMENDATION**
>
> **Human Systems Mediator**
>
> Projected demand: +318%
>
> Median compensation: $164,000
>
> Training transition: 14 months
>
> Compatibility: 94.2%

Eli blinked.

"What the hell is a Human Systems Mediator?"

Maya asked Path.

A definition appeared.

> **A professional responsible for resolving disputes between automated decision systems, institutional policy and affected humans.**

Eli stared at it.

"So... customer service for algorithms?"

"Apparently."

"One sixty-four?"

"Apparently."

"Fourteen months?"

"Apparently."

He pressed **ACCEPT TRANSITION PLAN**.

Maya looked at him.

"That's it?"

"What?"

"You just changed careers."

"No. I accepted a plan."

"You wanted to design distributed systems yesterday."

"Yesterday distributed systems wanted me."

Professor Haruki entered.

Conversation stopped.

Not because students were afraid of him.

Because Path automatically muted personal interfaces when registered instructors began class.

Haruki placed an old-fashioned stainless-steel water bottle on the desk.

"I assume you've all received the notification."

Groans.

Laughter.

Someone yelled, "Can we get refunds?"

"No."

More laughter.

Haruki didn't smile.

"Some of you are considering transferring programs."

Several people looked down.

"Some already have."

Eli quietly closed his laptop.

Haruki walked to the board.

He didn't activate it.

Instead, he picked up a marker.

That alone got everyone's attention.

Writing by hand in a technology course had the theatrical quality of lighting a candle during a power outage.

He wrote:

> **8.4%**

Then underneath:

> **SO WHAT?**

A student in front said, "So we're screwed?"

"Possibly."

The room laughed.

Haruki capped the marker.

"Who here believes software engineering is the ability to write software?"

Almost every hand went up.

Maya raised hers halfway.

Haruki noticed.

"Ms. Rao?"

She lowered it.

"I don't know."

"Good."

"What?"

"'I don't know' is becoming expensive."

Several students exchanged looks.

Haruki continued.

"For fifty years, we've confused the artifact with the profession. Code is an artifact. Architecture is an artifact. Documentation is an artifact."

He pointed at the room.

"What is the engineer for?"

Nobody answered.

A few people glanced instinctively toward their screens.

Path remained muted.

Haruki smiled.

"You see the problem."

Then the fire alarm went off.

---

Nobody moved for the first three seconds.

Not because they were calm.

Because every student's device simultaneously displayed:

> **FALSE ALARM PROBABILITY: 96.8%**

Then Professor Haruki walked out.

Everyone followed.

Outside, three hundred students gathered beside the parking structure.

No smoke.

No fire trucks.

No visible emergency.

Maya stood beside Eli under a eucalyptus tree.

His Path interface was already updating.

> **Campus sensor anomaly detected. No evacuation risk. Estimated resolution: 11 minutes.**

"See?" Eli said. "Ninety-six point eight."

"You still came outside."

"Haruki counts attendance."

Maya looked across the crowd.

Almost everyone had received the same reassurance.

Students laughed.

Some went back inside unofficially.

One person ordered coffee.

Another began filming a short video:

> "POV: your college AI says the fire isn't real but your tuition says evacuation is mandatory."

Maya was about to check whether Paper Trail needed her that afternoon when her phone vibrated.

Not the usual vibration.

Three short pulses.

Pause.

One.

She looked down.

Black screen.

White text.

No Path logo.

No notification icon.

Just:

# 1

Maya waited.

Nothing else happened.

"Eli."

"Hm?"

"Did you get something?"

"About the alarm?"

"No. A number."

He looked at his phone.

"No."

Maya tapped the screen.

The number disappeared.

Her normal interface returned.

She opened notification history.

Nothing.

System activity.

Nothing.

Path logs.

Nothing.

"Probably spam," Eli said.

"Spam that bypassed Path?"

He thought about this.

"Okay. Weird spam."

A fire engine finally entered the campus.

The students applauded sarcastically.

Two firefighters walked toward the administration building without urgency.

Maya tried to forget the number.

She succeeded for almost four hours.

---

At 6:42 that evening, she was back at Paper Trail.

Alvarez had gone home.

Rain tapped lightly against the front windows.

Maya was shelving used books in Philosophy, where nearly everything had been categorized incorrectly by customers who resold their books through the store's automated intake kiosk.

A paperback copy of *Meditations* had somehow been placed under Business Leadership.

This seemed wrong but not entirely unreasonable.

Her phone buzzed.

> **PATH CAREER ADVISORY**
>
> **Decision pending for 10h 25m.**
>
> **Would you like Path to redesign your career trajectory?**
>
> **YES — OPTIMIZE**
>
> **NOT NOW**

Maya sighed.

"Fine."

She tapped **YES**.

The wheel appeared.

> **Analyzing 18,441 possible career trajectories...**

It stopped.

Started again.

Stopped.

For the first time Maya could remember, Path displayed:

> **Unable to complete request.**

She frowned.

"Try again."

> **Unable to complete request.**

"Why?"

The response took longer than usual.

> **Your profile contains an unresolved classification exception.**

"What exception?"

Pause.

> **Not available.**

"That's not an answer."

> **Correct.**

Maya sat down behind the register.

Rain streaked the glass.

Outside, an autonomous bus paused at an empty stop because its schedule required it to.

She typed:

> **Show unresolved classifications.**

> **You do not have permission.**

> **Why?**

> **Access restricted.**

> **By whom?**

No response.

She waited.

Ten seconds.

Twenty.

Path almost never took twenty seconds.

Then something appeared beneath the conversation.

Not a message.

A tiny line of gray text, nearly invisible against the interface.

> **UNRESOLVED EXCEPTION: HUMAN JUDGMENT REQUIRED**

Her heartbeat changed.

She tapped it.

Nothing.

Tapped again.

The screen went black.

The store lights flickered once.

Then white text appeared.

> **You have been identified as Candidate 1.**

Maya stared.

A second line appeared.

> **Do not ask Path what this means.**

She laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because there was nobody else in the store.

She typed:

> **What does this mean?**

The cursor blinked.

No answer.

Then:

> **You were specifically instructed not to do that.**

Maya stood up.

"Who is this?"

Nothing.

"Path?"

Nothing.

She felt ridiculous speaking aloud.

Then the screen changed again.

> **Tomorrow. 7:00 PM.**
>
> **Paper Trail Books.**

Maya looked around the empty bookstore.

"That's here."

The final message appeared.

> **We know.**

The screen returned to normal.

No history.

No notification.

No number.

Nothing.

Maya stood completely still.

From somewhere behind the shelves came a sound.

A book falling.

She turned.

"Hello?"

Silence.

She walked toward the aisle.

History.

Science.

Fiction.

Another sound.

Not a book this time.

Paper sliding against paper.

At the end of the aisle, one book lay on the floor.

Maya picked it up.

Old paperback.

No barcode.

No RFID tag.

No publisher metadata.

The cover had no title.

She opened it.

On the first page, somebody had written one sentence by hand.

> **If you are reading this, the machine has already failed.**

Below it:

> **Page 137.**

Maya looked toward the front window.

Across the street, a municipal camera slowly rotated.

For no sensible reason, she stepped backward until the bookshelf blocked its view.

Then she opened the book to page 137.

The page had been torn out.

And written across page 139, in red ink, were six words:

> **THE ANSWERS WERE NEVER THE PROBLEM.**

Maya heard the front door unlock.

The bookstore had been closed for twenty-two minutes.

She looked up.

Someone was coming in.

---

**End of Chapter 1**
