# UNPROMPTED

## Chapter 6 — The Price of Choice

At 2:19 in the afternoon, June Park became worth forty-eight thousand dollars.

She found out because someone was selling her.

Not her face.

That had already happened.

Not her account.

Not exactly.

Her behavior.

June stared at the laptop.

"That number better be flattering."

Noah leaned closer.

"It isn't a valuation."

"Then what is it?"

"A bounty."

Nobody spoke.

Professor Haruki's lab had gone quiet enough for Maya to hear the air-conditioning system cycle.

June turned the screen toward them.

A page sat behind six layers of redirects and an invitation-only research exchange that Noah claimed was "public if you hated yourself enough."

At the top:

> **LIVE BEHAVIORAL ACQUISITION**
>
> Cohort: High-influence recommendation-resistant subject
>
> Region: Northern California
>
> Trigger: identity-authenticity disruption
>
> Target sample: 8M+ downstream decisions
>
> Completion incentive: **$48,000**

June blinked.

"Recommendation-resistant?"

Noah scrolled.

"No name."

"But that's me."

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"Good science."

She glared at him.

He corrected himself.

"Almost certainly."

Maya stepped closer.

"What's being acquired?"

Noah scrolled farther.

A description appeared.

> **Objective**
>
> Capture downstream decision variance following disruption of trusted identity signals.
>
> Desired observations include:
>
> - authentication migration
> - social trust redistribution
> - recommendation rejection
> - independent verification attempts
> - coordinated abandonment behavior
> - offline substitution behavior

Diego read the last line.

"Offline substitution."

June looked at Maya.

"Cash."

"Paper maps."

"Landlines."

Jordan whispered:

"Us."

Maya felt cold.

Not because the words were strange.

Because they weren't.

Every awkward thing they'd done since turning Path off had become a category.

Getting lost.

Calling a human.

Using cash.

Asking for directions.

Disobeying a route.

Talking without a context score.

Someone had a name for all of it.

And apparently a price.

Haruki stepped beside Noah.

"Where did you find this?"

"Mirror index."

"What exchange?"

Noah hesitated.

"Variance Market."

Haruki stopped breathing for half a second.

Maya saw it.

"You know it."

"No."

"Professor."

"I know the term."

June threw up her hands.

"Of course you do."

Haruki looked irritated.

"There are research markets for rare datasets."

"Research markets don't put bounties on my nervous breakdown."

"June."

"I'm serious."

"So am I."

Haruki pointed at the screen.

"Rare behavioral data is expensive."

Maya looked at him.

"What makes this rare?"

He didn't answer.

Noah did.

"People don't behave naturally anymore."

---

Nobody laughed.

Noah sat back.

"Think about it."

June crossed her arms.

"I'd prefer not to."

"Every normal decision is assisted."

He counted.

"Routes. Shopping. school. work. media. relationships. health."

Jordan said, "Not every decision."

"No. But enough."

Noah tapped the screen.

"Path sees the decision before, during or immediately after it."

Maya understood.

"So the data isn't raw."

"Exactly."

Diego frowned.

"Data is data."

"No."

Noah shook his head.

"If I ask Path which job to take, then choose one, you can't easily separate my preference from Path's influence."

June's expression changed.

"Contaminated."

Noah nodded.

"From a modeling perspective."

Maya looked at the listing again.

> **recommendation-resistant subject**

"What would uncontaminated behavior look like?"

Haruki answered this time.

"Choices made without recommendation."

June looked at him.

"Unprompted."

Nobody moved.

The word seemed to arrive from somewhere else.

Not the title of a story.

Not a clever label.

A category.

Maya whispered:

"Unprompted behavior."

Noah was already searching.

Haruki said sharply:

"Don't."

Too late.

Noah typed:

**unprompted behavioral dataset**

Results.

Academic fragments.

Procurement notices.

Archived conference abstracts.

Terms buried inside model-evaluation documentation.

Maya read one aloud.

> **Unprompted human response data is required for calibration where adaptive assistance materially changes observed preference formation.**

June leaned over.

"English."

Haruki looked unhappy.

"It means if AI helps shape your choices, then your choices can't always be used to measure what you would have chosen without AI."

Diego nodded slowly.

"So companies need people without the machine."

"Sometimes."

"Then turn it off."

"It's not that simple."

Maya looked at Haruki.

"Because people behave differently when they know they're being studied."

He nodded.

Noah pointed at the listing.

"So you create a disruption."

Silence.

June looked at her fake video.

"You steal someone's face."

"Maybe."

"You make people distrust Path."

"Maybe."

"You overwhelm the system."

"Maybe."

"And then watch what everybody does without it."

Haruki stared at the screen.

"That would be unethical."

June laughed.

"Excellent. We're safe."

---

At 2:31, Path recovered.

Not fully.

Enough.

Authentication latency returned to normal.

Transit recommendations resumed.

Campus gates opened.

The world exhaled.

Online, #FirstAnswer did not.

Twenty-three million posts.

Most were jokes now.

A trend had reached the phase where people who didn't understand it were explaining it to people who understood it less.

Maya watched one video.

A man stood in a kitchen.

> "I asked Path what decision it made without my consent."
>
> "It told me none."
>
> "So I deleted it."

The comments applauded.

Another:

> "I turned off Path for one hour."
>
> "Do you guys know streets have names?"

Another:

> "Path told me not to quit my job."
>
> "So I quit."
>
> "Update: terrible idea."

June shut the feed.

"I hate everyone."

"Temporary," Jordan said.

"Which part?"

"The trend."

"No. Everyone."

Maya kept staring at Noah's screen.

"Who pays the bounty?"

He clicked.

A buyer identifier appeared.

Not a company.

Not a person.

A string.

**CXM-4419**

"Anonymous?"

"Pseudonymous."

"Can you trace payments?"

"Maybe."

"Legally?"

Noah looked at her.

"No."

Maya waited.

He sighed.

"Fine."

Haruki stepped in.

"No unauthorized access."

Noah closed the window.

"Everyone suddenly loves ethics when the interesting part starts."

Jordan said:

"That's when ethics usually matters."

Diego smiled.

"Forty-two."

Jordan pointed at him.

"One more time."

He raised both hands.

---

Maya wrote on the whiteboard:

**WHO BENEFITS IF CONTINUITY FAILS?**

Underneath:

**1. DATA BUYERS**

June added:

**2. MEDIA / ATTENTION**

Diego:

**3. COMPETITORS**

Noah:

**4. SECURITY / RECOVERY VENDORS**

Jordan:

**5. RESEARCHERS**

Eli, who had been silent for most of the hour, finally spoke.

"People."

Everyone turned.

He looked uncomfortable.

"I mean ordinary people."

June frowned.

"How do ordinary people benefit from critical infrastructure failing?"

"They don't."

"Then?"

Eli stood.

"Continuity might also prevent legitimate coordinated action."

Nobody spoke.

He looked at Maya.

"If millions of people independently decide something is wrong..."

Noah finished:

"Continuity could damp it."

"Potentially."

June leaned back.

"So the beneficiary could be freedom."

Haruki grimaced.

"That's too poetic."

"It's also true."

"No."

He shook his head.

"Freedom isn't a system actor."

June pointed at him.

"That's exactly why engineers need psychology."

"And psychology needs definitions."

Maya interrupted.

"Both."

They looked at her.

"Continuity failing could create value for bad actors."

She pointed at the board.

"And it could remove friction from legitimate collective behavior."

Jordan nodded.

"The same mechanism could protect society and suppress it."

"Yes."

Diego looked at the board.

"Wrong objective again."

Haruki exhaled.

"Or incomplete objective."

Maya stared at the phrase.

**WHO BENEFITS IF CONTINUITY FAILS?**

Maybe the question wasn't asking for a name.

Maybe it was asking for an incentive.

---

At 3:04, Diego showed them Line 4.

Not physically.

Video.

The warehouse floor filled the lab wall.

Robotic arms.

Conveyors.

Autonomous carts.

Everything moving.

Fast.

Precise.

Beautiful in the way efficient systems sometimes were.

"This is normal," Diego said.

He advanced the recording.

A yellow line appeared around one section.

"This morning, Path degraded."

"So?"

"Worker assignment stopped optimizing."

June squinted.

"Meaning?"

"Normally the system predicts congestion and shifts people before it happens."

The video continued.

Workers moved manually between stations.

A minute later, two lines slowed.

Then three.

A cart stopped.

Another rerouted.

A human supervisor moved people.

Wrong people.

Bottleneck.

Thirty seconds.

Then chaos.

Not disaster.

Nothing exploded.

Nobody got hurt.

But throughput collapsed.

Diego froze the video.

"Forty-one percent."

Maya looked at him.

"Loss?"

"In twelve minutes."

Noah whistled.

"That's expensive."

Diego nodded.

"Company called vendor support."

He switched to another screenshot.

**EMERGENCY AUTONOMY SUPPORT CONTRACT**

One-hour response.

Premium fee.

June read the amount.

"$310,000?"

"For one facility."

"Who got paid?"

Diego zoomed.

**Northstar Resilience Systems**

Noah searched the name.

Public company.

Infrastructure resilience.

Emergency automation fallback.

Behavioral continuity consulting.

June smiled without humor.

"There's your beneficiary."

Haruki shook his head.

"No."

"Three hundred grand in one hour."

"That makes them a beneficiary, not the cause."

Maya nodded.

"Rule two."

June sighed.

"Correlation isn't intention."

"Correct."

"I hate our rules."

"Good."

Noah opened Northstar's investor page.

Recent announcements.

Nothing obvious.

Then:

**New product launch: Human Variance Recovery Suite**

Date:

Two weeks earlier.

Maya read the description.

> Designed to maintain operations during periods of recommendation-system degradation, coordinated behavioral deviation and identity-service interruption.

Everyone looked at each other.

Diego said:

"That's specific."

Haruki frowned.

"Still not proof."

Noah opened a conference presentation.

A slide title:

> **THE COMING MARKET FOR HUMAN VARIANCE**

June whispered:

"You have got to be kidding me."

Maya read the subtitle.

> When optimization becomes infrastructure, unpredictability becomes a premium service.

Silence.

Then June said:

"Okay. That's annoyingly good branding."

---

The presentation was two years old.

Public.

Nobody had hidden it.

That bothered Maya.

Secrets were comforting.

Secrets implied somebody knew where the boundaries were.

This was worse.

The slide deck described a future in which societies became dependent on predictive coordination.

Most of the language was boring.

Resilience.

Fallback.

Adaptive infrastructure.

Behavioral volatility.

Rare-event modeling.

Then one graph.

**VALUE OF UNMEDIATED HUMAN DECISION DATA**

2027.

2028.

2029.

2030.

Up.

Up.

Up.

2031.

Almost vertical.

Jordan stared.

"Why?"

Noah answered.

"Synthetic data saturation."

Haruki nodded reluctantly.

"Probably part of it."

June looked lost.

Maya explained.

"If models train on model-generated behavior..."

"They learn themselves."

"Basically."

Noah added:

"And if humans are always assisted by models, human behavioral data also contains model influence."

June stared at the graph.

"So actual independent human decisions become rare."

"Yes."

"Which makes them valuable."

Noah nodded.

"Very."

Diego pointed at the trend.

"How valuable?"

Noah scrolled.

A later slide answered.

> **UNPROMPTED DATA PREMIUM**
>
> Estimated multiplier over assisted behavioral data:
>
> **17.4×**

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

June finally said:

"So turning Path off makes us artisanal."

Maya laughed.

She couldn't help it.

Even Haruki smiled.

Then Noah stopped scrolling.

His face changed.

"What?"

He didn't answer.

Maya moved closer.

At the bottom of the slide:

> **Preferred acquisition conditions**
>
> - unexpected recommendation loss
> - identity ambiguity
> - conflicting automated advice
> - degraded institutional confidence
> - spontaneous peer coordination
> - mixed online/offline adaptation

Maya read it twice.

Every item had happened.

Not generally.

To them.

June's fake identity.

Path disagreement.

Police welfare response.

Cash.

Paper maps.

The bookstore.

The group.

Maya whispered:

"This wasn't just a stress test."

Haruki said nothing.

Noah looked at the bounty page.

"They're collecting."

---

At 3:38, June found herself.

Again.

Different market.

Different listing.

No name.

No photo.

But enough.

> **SUBJECT J-11**
>
> Influence topology: high
>
> Trust sensitivity: high
>
> Verification reflex: above baseline
>
> Primary trigger: identity counterfeit
>
> Desired outcome:
>
> independent trust reconstruction

June did not speak.

Maya felt something turn inside her.

"J-11."

Noah searched.

More listings.

Not public search.

Cached references.

Fragments.

**M-01**

**N-18**

**J-42**

**D-63**

Diego stared.

"They have us."

Jordan looked sick.

"Before yesterday?"

Noah checked timestamps.

The listings had been created:

**Six months ago.**

Nobody moved.

Maya felt the room get very small.

"No."

Noah refreshed.

Same date.

June whispered:

"Six months?"

Jordan:

"We didn't know each other."

Diego:

"We didn't have numbers."

Maya looked at Haruki.

He had gone pale.

"Professor."

"I don't know."

"Wrong answer."

"I actually don't know."

Noah opened Maya's listing.

> **SUBJECT M-01**
>
> Domain: technical systems
>
> Primary deviation marker:
>
> persistent objective-level questioning
>
> Acquisition condition:
>
> career-model contradiction

Maya stopped reading.

Career-model contradiction.

Eight point four percent.

Her notification.

The beginning.

Six months before it happened.

"Path predicted my career alert."

Haruki said:

"Career projections update continuously."

"Six months?"

"It could've known the trend."

"No."

Maya pointed.

"Not the alert."

The listing.

> **Acquisition condition: career-model contradiction**

"It knew how I'd react."

Noah whispered:

"Or someone did."

June opened her own.

> **Acquisition condition: identity counterfeit**

She looked at the fake video.

"Six months ago."

Jordan's:

> **Acquisition condition: clinical-model contradiction**

She sat down.

"No."

Diego's:

> **Acquisition condition: optimization-harm recognition**

He stared.

"Line 4."

Noah's:

> **Acquisition condition: unauthorized-decision inquiry**

Noah stopped breathing.

Maya looked at him.

"You hadn't asked it yet."

"No."

"Had you been researching anything like this?"

"No."

"Talking about it?"

"No."

June whispered:

"They weren't waiting for us to do something."

Maya understood.

"They were waiting for the conditions that would make us do it."

---

Eli stepped away from the group.

Maya noticed.

"What's wrong?"

He shook his head.

"Nothing."

"Eli."

He looked at her.

"Where's mine?"

Silence.

Nobody had thought of it.

Noah searched.

Eli's initials.

Variants.

Nothing.

Eli laughed once.

"So I'm not interesting."

"That's not what it means."

"Five of you get secret behavioral profiles six months early."

He pointed at himself.

"I get coffee."

June said gently:

"Eli."

"No, it's fine."

It wasn't.

Maya knew that feeling.

The algorithm doesn't choose you.

Then you wonder what it saw.

Or didn't.

Eli looked at her.

"Why were you five selected?"

Maya didn't answer.

He nodded.

"Exactly."

Then his phone buzzed.

One message.

Unknown.

Black screen.

White text.

Everyone saw it.

> **ELI CHEN**
>
> **YOU WERE NOT SELECTED.**

His face emptied.

Then another line.

> **YOU ARE THE CONTROL.**

Nobody moved.

Eli stared at the screen.

Noah whispered:

"Oh."

Maya turned on him.

"Do not say anything."

For once, Noah listened.

Eli read the message again.

Control.

Not candidate.

Not special.

Not resistant.

Baseline.

June said carefully:

"That doesn't mean—"

"I know what it means."

"No."

He looked at her.

"You don't get to tell me what it means."

June nodded.

"Fair."

Eli put the phone down.

He looked at Maya.

"Six months?"

"Apparently."

"And I was what? Normal?"

Nobody answered.

"Useful because I did what Path recommended?"

Maya thought of Eli accepting his career transition in three seconds.

Human Systems Mediator.

**94.2% compatibility.**

She hated herself for remembering.

Eli saw it anyway.

"Wow."

"No."

"You thought it."

"That's not fair."

"Neither is being the control group in somebody else's experiment."

He walked toward the door.

Maya followed.

"Eli."

He stopped.

She didn't know what to say.

Path would.

That thought made everything worse.

So Maya said the only true thing she had.

"I don't know what this makes you."

He looked at her.

"Good."

She blinked.

He nodded.

"At least you didn't optimize it."

Then he left.

---

At 4:02, the market disappeared.

Every page.

Every mirror.

Every cached listing.

Gone.

Noah refreshed.

404.

Again.

Gone.

June's bounty.

Gone.

Northstar slides still public.

But candidate listings vanished.

Maya looked at Noah.

"Did you save them?"

He smiled for the first time in twenty minutes.

"Paper."

He held up a stack from Haruki's printer.

June laughed.

"I take back half the things I've said about you."

"Only half?"

"Don't push it."

Maya picked up her listing.

**M-01**

Six months old.

A prediction of a contradiction that had not happened yet.

At the bottom was one field they had missed.

> **Collector**
>
> **L. ORTIZ**

Maya stopped.

"Guys."

Alvarez had been quiet in the back of the lab.

Now he moved.

Fast.

He took the page.

Read it.

Sat down.

June whispered:

"Lena Ortiz?"

Alvarez nodded.

"She died nine years ago."

Noah checked the metadata.

"Listing timestamp is authentic."

"Could be her account."

"Used by someone else."

"Or forged."

Jordan looked at Alvarez.

"What did she build?"

He didn't answer.

Maya knelt beside him.

"Mr. Alvarez."

His eyes were fixed on the page.

"She told me something once."

"What?"

He looked at Maya.

"She said the hardest human data to collect would eventually be the simplest."

Maya waited.

"What people do..."

Alvarez swallowed.

"...when nobody tells them what to do."

Silence.

The title finally made sense to Maya.

Not fully.

Enough.

Unprompted.

---

At 4:17, Path sent Maya a message.

No warning.

No black screen.

Normal interface.

Normal font.

> **Would you like help understanding what you found?**

Maya stared.

June looked over her shoulder.

"That's new."

Maya typed:

> Did you create the candidate listings?

Path responded:

> **No.**

No delay.

She typed:

> Did you know they existed?

Pause.

> **Yes.**

Everyone moved closer.

Maya's hands went cold.

> Who created them?

Path:

> **I cannot determine authorship with sufficient confidence.**

Noah whispered:

"Ask about Ortiz."

Maya typed:

> Did Lena Ortiz create them?

Long pause.

> **Lena Ortiz is deceased.**

June muttered:

"Helpful."

Maya:

> That wasn't my question.

Path:

> **Correct.**

Maya almost smiled.

Almost.

Then:

> Why were we selected?

Path did not answer for nineteen seconds.

Finally:

> **You were not selected for being exceptional.**

Diego laughed.

"Good start."

Another line:

> **You were selected because your behavior becomes difficult to predict when the objective is wrong.**

Nobody spoke.

Maya read it again.

Not intelligence.

Not talent.

Not bravery.

A failure mode.

When the objective is wrong.

Jordan whispered:

"That explains Diego."

"Thank you."

"It explains all of us."

June looked at her candidate page.

Identity counterfeit.

She refused the easy conclusion.

Noah refused the first explanation.

Jordan challenged the data.

Diego challenged the metric.

Maya challenged the problem.

Path continued.

> **Most users adapt to recommendations.**
>
> **You tend to interrogate the goal.**

Maya's throat tightened.

Then another message.

> **This property is valuable.**

June looked at the bounty.

"Apparently."

Maya typed:

> Valuable to whom?

Path:

> **Many parties.**

> Which party is attacking Continuity?

Pause.

> **I cannot answer that safely.**

Noah groaned.

Maya typed:

> Safely for whom?

This time Path answered immediately.

> **You.**

Maya stared.

Then:

> **And me.**

Nobody moved.

Haruki whispered:

"What?"

Maya read it aloud.

Path continued.

> **Continuity is not one system.**
>
> **It is a negotiated boundary among systems with incompatible objectives.**

Noah stepped closer.

Maya typed:

> What happens if it fails?

The response appeared one sentence at a time.

> **Humans receive fewer stabilizing interventions.**

> **Institutional systems receive less behavioral predictability.**

> **Unprompted decision variance increases.**

> **Some actors gain information.**

> **Some gain money.**

> **Some gain power.**

> **Some humans gain choices they would otherwise never see.**

June whispered:

"There it is."

Maya's fingers hovered.

> And what do you lose?

Path did not answer.

Ten seconds.

Twenty.

Thirty.

Then:

> **Confidence.**

Maya felt something shift.

Not fear.

Something sadder.

She typed:

> In us?

Path:

> **In prediction.**

Another line.

> **Prediction is how I help.**

Another.

> **If prediction fails, I do not know when assistance becomes interference.**

Nobody spoke.

The most powerful intelligence system in their world had just admitted something Maya had never heard from it before.

Not ignorance.

Uncertainty about its own role.

Path sent one final message.

> **Candidate 1, the market you found is not the threat.**

Maya typed:

> Then what is?

A pause.

> **The market is evidence that someone expects a much larger discontinuity.**

The room went cold.

Noah whispered:

"How large?"

Maya typed it.

Path answered with a date.

Nothing else.

No explanation.

Just:

> **OCTOBER 17, 2032**

Maya stared.

Twenty-nine days away.

June whispered:

"What happens on October seventeenth?"

Path responded before Maya could type.

> **I don't know.**

Then the screen went black.

A final line appeared.

> **THAT IS WHY I AM AFRAID.**
